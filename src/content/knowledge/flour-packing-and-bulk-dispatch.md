The packing line is where a flour mill meets its customers, and it is routinely the least engineered part of the plant.

Buyers spend months on flow sheets and sifter area, then accept whatever packer is in the quotation. The consequences appear as commercial problems — giveaway, complaints, blocked loading bays — which nobody traces back to a scale that was specified in an afternoon.

## Giveaway is silent and it is large

A packing scale with a ±2% error on 25 kg means some bags at 24.5 kg and some at 25.5 kg. Regulatory tolerance is usually one-sided — you may give more, never less — so a mill running a ±2% scale ends up setting the target at 25.5 kg to stay legal.

That is 500 g of free flour per bag. On a line running 200 bags an hour, eight hours a day, 300 days a year, it is over 240 tonnes of flour given away annually. At USD 400 a tonne, that is close to USD 100,000.

| Scale accuracy on 25 kg | Target needed to stay legal | Annual giveaway at 200 bags/h |
|---|---|---|
| ±2% (volumetric) | 25.5 kg | ~240 t |
| ±1% | 25.25 kg | ~120 t |
| ±0.5% (load cell) | 25.13 kg | ~60 t |
| ±0.2% (net weigher) | 25.05 kg | ~24 t |

The difference in capital cost between the top and bottom of that table is a few tens of thousands of dollars. The difference in annual giveaway is a hundred thousand. It is one of the few clearly positive returns in the whole plant, and it is routinely skipped because the packer is bought last, against a budget already spent.

## The finished flour silo is a buffer, not storage

A mill with no finished flour silo has coupled its milling rate to its packing rate. When the bag line stops — a seal failure, a pallet jam, a truck that has not arrived — the mill stops too, and restarting means cleaning spouts and discarding the first flour through.

A silo holding 8–12 hours of output decouples them. It also lets you run the mill at its efficient continuous rate instead of matching a variable packing demand.

Sizing rule that works: **half a day of output, minimum.** A [60 t/d](/flour-milling-plant/40-60tpd-flour-plant/) plant wants 30 tonnes of finished capacity. Below 40 t/d, a surge bin of 2–4 hours is usually enough, because the mill is flexible and labour is shared.

Two design points that decide whether the silo helps or hurts:

**Discharge geometry.** Flour is cohesive and bridges. A cone angle below 60° from horizontal, or a flat-bottomed silo with an air pad, is the difference between reliable discharge and a man with a mallet.

**Aeration and moisture.** Flour leaving a silo at 40 °C from the mill will condense on cool silo walls. Insulate, or accept crusting on the wall and design access for cleaning it.

## Bulk is a different product

Bulk flour dispatch — tanker loading into a customer's pneumatic receiving system — is not bagged flour in a bigger container.

Flour is aerated during pneumatic transfer, which changes its bulk density and flow behaviour. A flour that packs beautifully into 25 kg bags can bridge in a customer's receiving bin, and the complaint arrives as "your flour does not flow" rather than as a specification failure.

The practical implications:

- Bulk customers need consistent bulk density, tested the way they receive it
- Tanker loading needs a loading arm, a dust collection point and a weighbridge — a bay, not a hose
- You need aeration capability at the silo discharge, not just gravity
- Segregation matters more: a bulk tanker cannot be partly loaded with a different grade

## What I would specify

A load-cell net weigher with ±0.5% or better, a seal integrity check on every bag rather than a sample, and a date coder that cannot be skipped. Then a finished flour silo sized at half a day, with a discharge cone steep enough to actually empty.

The rest — palletisers, shrink wrappers, robotic stacking — is labour economics. Worth it at high volume and high wage, not worth it at 20 t/d. The three items above are not labour economics. They are the difference between shipping the product you made and shipping a complaint.
