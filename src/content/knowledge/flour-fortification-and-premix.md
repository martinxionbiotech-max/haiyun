Fortification looks like a simple engineering problem — add a small quantity of premix to a large quantity of flour — and it is almost always approached that way. Then the first laboratory check fails, or the first shipment is rejected at the border, and it turns out the problem was regulatory rather than mechanical.

## The regulation names compounds

This is the single most expensive misunderstanding in fortification projects.

A regulation that says "iron" does not mean any iron. Ferrous sulfate, ferrous fumarate, electrolytically reduced iron and ferric pyrophosphate have different bioavailability, different colour effects, different cost and different permitted levels. A mill that buys "iron premix" and gets the wrong compound can be perfectly compliant on the certificate and non-compliant on the assay.

| Element | Common compounds | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Iron | Ferrous sulfate, fumarate, reduced iron, NaFeEDTA | Bioavailability and colour differ by compound, not by level |
| Folic acid | Folic acid (single form) | Degrades with heat and storage — overage is normal |
| Vitamin B12 | Cyanocobalamin | Sensitive to light and pH; premix must be protected |
| Zinc | Zinc oxide, zinc sulfate | Oxide is cheaper, less bioavailable |
| Vitamin A | Retinyl palmitate | Borderline; used where deficiency is severe |

The practical rule: get the standard in writing from the authority that will test your flour, and buy premix against that standard rather than against a supplier's catalogue description.

## Where the dosing equipment goes

Premix is added to the finished flour stream, downstream of the sifter and upstream of the [packing line](/knowledge/flour-packing-and-bulk-dispatch/). Two locations are common:

**At the collector or the flour conveyor inlet.** Best for distribution, because the conveying that follows does some mixing.

**Immediately before the finished flour silo.** Acceptable if the silo has an agitator or if the filling pattern provides mixing. A silo filled from a single point does not mix; it stratifies.

What does not work is gravity feeding premix into a falling stream with no mixing downstream. The premix is 0.1–0.3% of the mass. It will not distribute itself.

| Feeder type | Accuracy | Cost | When to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Volumetric screw | ±3–5% | Low | Small mills, where the standard allows overage |
| Volumetric belt | ±2–3% | Low-mid | Common in 20–60 t/d plants |
| Gravimetric loss-in-weight | ±0.5–1% | High | Any plant subject to laboratory verification |

For a mill that will be tested against a legal limit, the gravimetric feeder is the right answer. The cost difference is a few thousand dollars against the cost of a rejected shipment.

## The two failure modes that actually happen

**The feeder stops and nobody knows.** A bridged premix hopper, a worn screw, a control interlock — the feeder stops while the mill keeps running. Flour continues to the packing line unfortified, and the sample taken at the end of the shift is the first evidence.

The control is a run-signal interlock: if the premix feeder is not running, the flour stream stops or diverts. It is a relay and a level sensor, and it is the single most valuable item in the fortification installation.

**Overage compounding.** Folic acid degrades in storage and through baking, so regulations permit adding more than the declared label level. Mills sometimes respond by adding more than the permitted overage as well. That is a compliance failure, not caution — a flour containing 160% of the declared level fails just as surely as one at 60%.

The control is a written dosing record against the standard's overage allowance, checked monthly against laboratory results. It is paperwork, and it is the difference between a system and a habit.

## Laboratory verification, and what it catches

Finished flour is tested for the added nutrients — typically iron by colorimetric or ICP method, and folic acid by microbiological assay or HPLC.

Two things worth knowing about the results. First, iron assays measure total iron, and wheat flour already contains iron, so the baseline must be subtracted — a mill without a baseline figure cannot interpret its own results. Second, folic acid results in flour are routinely 10–20% below the target even when dosing is correct, because of degradation between fortification and analysis. That is expected, and the standard's overage allowance accounts for it.

## The commercial framing

Fortification adds cost per tonne and delivers no benefit the mill can charge for. In a mandatory market it is a licence to operate. In a voluntary market it is a marketing claim that requires the same discipline.

Either way, the mills that get into trouble are not the ones that spent least on the equipment. They are the ones that treated a regulatory requirement as a purchase order.
