## What ash physically is

Burn flour at 550–600 °C and the organic matter — starch, protein, fibre, fat — oxidises away. What remains is mineral ash: potassium, phosphorus, magnesium, calcium, iron, zinc and traces of others.

Endosperm contains about 0.4% minerals. Bran contains about 8%. That 20× difference is what makes ash a usable measurement: **ash is a mineral-based measure of how much bran is in your flour.**

## Specifications by market

| Market | Patent flour | Standard flour |
|---|---|---|
| European | 0.50–0.60% | 0.70–0.85% |
| North American | 0.48–0.55% | 0.65–0.80% |
| Middle East | 0.55–0.70% | 0.75–0.95% |
| South Asia (Maida) | 0.45–0.58% | 0.70–0.90% |
| Southeast Asia | 0.50–0.65% | 0.70–0.88% |
| Africa | 0.60–0.75% | 0.80–1.00% |

## Why it decides price

Premium buyers — industrial bakeries, noodle manufacturers, export customers — specify ash because it predicts behaviour:

| Ash | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Low (0.50%) | Pale colour, high starch purity, consistent fermentation |
| Medium (0.65%) | Slightly darker, more flavour, lower cost |
| High (0.85%) | Noticeably darker, shorter shelf life, cheap |

A bakery buying 20 t/month will pay 4–8% more for 0.52% ash than for 0.62%. That 8% on a 60 t/d plant at USD 400/t is roughly USD 700 per day.

## Testing procedure

1. Pre-weigh a clean, dry crucible and record the mass
2. Weigh 2–5 g of flour into it and record
3. Incinerate at 550–600 °C for 3–5 hours
4. Cool in a desiccator (not in open air — ash absorbs moisture)
5. Weigh
6. Ash % = (ash mass ÷ dry flour mass) × 100

Run in duplicate. If the two results differ by more than 0.02 points, repeat.

## Tracing a rise: the stream method

When total flour ash rises, do not guess. Sample each stream and test:

| Stream | Typical ash | If it rises, check |
|---|---|---|
| 1B flour | 0.45–0.55% | Break roll flutes, sifter screen |
| 2B flour | 0.55–0.70% | Break roll condition |
| 3B flour | 0.70–0.90% | Break roll condition, screen integrity |
| 1M flour | 0.42–0.52% | Feed purity, purifier air balance |
| 3M flour | 0.50–0.60% | Sifter screen, detacher |
| Tailings | 0.90–1.30% | Tailing passage settings |
| Blend | Per spec | Weighted average |

If only one stream is out, you have found the machine. If all streams are uniformly higher, the problem is upstream: wheat quality, cleaning, or tempering.

## The three most common causes

**1. Worn break roll flutes (45% of cases).** Dull flutes polish bran into the flour instead of cutting it away. Check fluting hours against the 1,500–2,500 schedule.

**2. Torn or slack sifter screen (25%).** Oversize material leaks into the flour stream. Inspect frames weekly under strong light.

**3. Destoner inefficiency (15%).** Stones that reach the rolls create sparks and fine dust; more importantly, poor destoning correlates with poor overall cleaning. Run the 200-stone test quarterly.

The remaining 15%: purifier out of air balance, scourer with worn beaters, dampener drift causing over- or under-tempering.

## Ash versus extraction

These two move in opposite directions. Every point of extraction beyond about 78% adds bran and raises ash.

The correct target is not minimum ash. It is **maximum extraction at the specified ash**. A mill producing 74% extraction at 0.50% ash is underperforming; one producing 78% at 0.62% ash may be exactly right for its market.
