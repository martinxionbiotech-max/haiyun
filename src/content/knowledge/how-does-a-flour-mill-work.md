A flour mill does not grind wheat into powder. It opens the kernel gradually across nine to fifteen passages, scraping endosperm off bran a little at a time and sifting the result after every one — because the objective is separation, not size reduction. Everything else in the plant exists to support that sequence.

## The kernel you are trying to take apart

A wheat kernel is roughly:

| Component | Share of kernel | What it contains |
|---|---|---|
| Endosperm | 80–83% | Starch and protein — this is flour |
| Bran | 14–16% | Fibre, minerals, some protein |
| Germ | 2–3% | Oils, vitamins, enzymes |

The whole job of a flour mill is to separate the 80% endosperm from the 16% bran without dragging bran into the flour. That sounds simple. It is not, because the bran is glued directly onto the endosperm with no clean interface.

## Step 1 — Cleaning

Wheat arrives with stones, straw, dirt, broken kernels and metal. Every one of these either damages the machines downstream or ends up in your flour.

Six to nine machine stages remove them by size, density, surface abrasion and magnetism. See the cleaning handbook for detail.

## Step 2 — Tempering

Water is added and the wheat rests for 12–24 hours. The bran becomes leathery and the endosperm softens.

Without this step the bran shatters into dust during grinding and the flour turns grey.

## Step 3 — Breaking

The first roll passage — called **1B for first break** — runs fluted rolls at a large differential (2.5:1). The fast roll holds the kernel while the slow roll shears.

The kernel splits. Endosperm comes away in chunks. Nothing is reduced to flour yet, and that is deliberate: grinding everything at once would smear bran through the flour.

Output from 1B goes to a sifter, which separates:

- Fine flour (small fraction)
- Coarse middlings (go to break 2 or sizing)
- Bran with adherent endosperm (goes to break 2)

## Step 4 — More breaks

Break 2 runs the bran again with tighter rolls, scraping off more endosperm. Then break 3, break 4. Each passage recovers what the previous one missed.

After 4 breaks, bran still carries 6–8% residual starch. After 5, it drops to 4–6%.

## Step 5 — Reduction

Clean endosperm chunks from the sifters go to reduction passages. These run smooth rolls at a much smaller differential (1.25–2.0:1), gradually reducing particle size.

Reduction produces flour. Break passages produce the material that reduction works on.

## Step 6 — Sifting

Every passage output goes to a sifter. A [plansifter](/equipment/high-square-plansifter/) is a stack of frames with different mesh sizes, gyrating at 245 rpm.

It separates each stream into flour, coarse middlings, fine middlings and bran. The fine material goes forward; the coarse goes back to another passage.

## Step 7 — Purifying

Middlings of the same size but different purity are separated in a [purifier](/equipment/purifier/), which uses airflow over a vibrating deck. Pure endosperm is lighter and more angular, so it floats and travels. Bran-contaminated particles settle.

This is the machine that turns "well-sifted flour" into "low-ash patent flour".

## Step 8 — Packing

Flour is check-sifted one last time, magnets remove any ferrous material, and the packer fills bags to ±0.2% accuracy.

## What comes out

| Output | Share of wheat | Destination |
|---|---|---|
| Flour | 72–78% | Human food |
| Bran | 20–26% | Animal feed |
| Germ | 2–3% | Oil extraction or feed |
| Losses | 1–3% | Cleaning and dust |

## The thing that surprises most people

Flour milling does not grind wheat into powder. It **takes wheat apart**, one passage at a time, and each passage is designed to separate rather than to pulverise.

That is why a mill with more passages extracts more flour from the same wheat — and why a 4B5M flow sheet beats a 3B4M on economics even though it costs more.
