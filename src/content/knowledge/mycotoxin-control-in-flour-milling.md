Mycotoxins are the one contaminant in a flour mill that the process cannot fix, and the industry's habitual response — clean harder, mill around it, blend it down — does not work.

That is worth stating plainly because a great deal of milling practice assumes otherwise. Sellers of cleaning equipment will describe reductions. Quality managers will describe blending plans. Both are describing ways of managing an exposure you have already taken on.

The physics is simpler than the commercial conversation around it.

## Where it comes from, and why it is uneven

Fusarium head blight infects wheat in the field during flowering, in wet weather. The mycotoxin — usually deoxynivalenol, DON — stays in the kernel, concentrated in the bran and the germ.

Fungal growth during storage is a different pathway. Ochratoxin A and aflatoxin develop in poorly stored grain, in pockets of moisture, in bins with condensation or leaks.

Both share a characteristic that decides everything downstream: **the contamination is not evenly distributed.**

A silo that averages 900 µg/kg DON is not a silo of wheat at 900. It is mostly wheat at 300–500 with a small fraction of shrivelled, infected kernels at 10,000–30,000. Those kernels are lighter, so they float to the top when a bin is filled and travel differently on a conveyor.

Which means a probe sample can miss them entirely, and a composite can be dominated by them.

| Sampling situation | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Single probe from truck top | Almost nothing — samples the lightest fraction |
| 5–8 cores, mechanical probe, whole depth | A defensible lot estimate |
| Composite from a conveyor during unloading | The best practical figure |
| One laboratory result per silo | A number, not a measurement |

## What cleaning actually removes

Cleaning helps, and it is worth doing, and it will not save you.

The removal mechanism is physical: broken kernels, shrivelled kernels, dust and light material carry a disproportionate share of the toxin, and scouring removes surface contamination. Expect 10–25% reduction on a well-run line with good aspiration and a [scourer](/equipment/horizontal-wheat-scourer/).

What cleaning does not do is remove toxin from sound kernels, because it is inside them. No amount of [aspiration](/equipment/aspiration-separator/) reaches the endosperm of an infected grain.

## What milling does: concentrate it

This is the counter-intuitive part, and it is where expectations usually break.

Milling separates bran from endosperm. The bran holds most of the DON. So patent flour is *lower* than the wheat — typically 60–80% of the wheat level — and bran is far higher, often 200–400%.

But the arithmetic that matters is per tonne of output. Because bran is only 25% of the mill's output and flour is 75%, the flour leaving your mill carries more total DON than a naive reading suggests. And whole wheat flour concentrates everything the bran had, into a product that is worse than the wheat it was made from.

| Stream | Typical DON relative to wheat |
|---|---|
| Wheat intake | 1.0 |
| After cleaning | 0.75–0.90 |
| Patent flour | 0.60–0.80 |
| Clear / low-grade flour | 0.90–1.20 |
| Bran | 2.0–4.0 |
| Whole wheat flour | 1.1–1.4 |

The implication is uncomfortable and worth sitting with: **making whole wheat flour from marginal wheat makes the problem worse, not better.** If your market is whole wheat, your intake standard has to be stricter than a patent-flour mill's.

## The controls that work, in order

**First, reject at intake.** This is the only intervention with real leverage. A rapid test at the weighbridge — strip test or a benchtop reader — costs a few dollars per load and is the difference between a quality system and a complaint.

**Second, store it properly.** Ochratoxin and aflatoxin are storage problems, and storage problems are engineering problems: aeration, moisture control, no leaks, no condensation on the roof underside. The mills that get ochratoxin are the mills with a bin that has been leaking since the roof was built.

**Third, know your destination standard before you buy.** A load legal in one market and illegal in another is a commercial decision, not a technical one, and it belongs to the person who signs the purchase contract.

**Fourth, segregate.** If you must accept marginal wheat, mill it separately, dedicate the output to a product where it can legally go, and clean the line between runs. Blending is what people do instead of segregation, and it is the weaker option for the reason above: uneven distribution means the blend is not even.

## What I would tell a new mill owner

Buy a rapid test kit before you buy your first silo. It is the cheapest quality instrument in the plant and it protects the only thing you cannot recover — a batch of flour that has already shipped.

And be sceptical of any supplier who describes mycotoxin as a cleaning problem. It is an intake problem. Everything after the weighbridge is damage control.
