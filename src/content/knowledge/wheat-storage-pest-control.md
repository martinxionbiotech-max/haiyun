Insects need grain above 20 °C and 13% moisture, which makes cooling below 15 °C a physical insecticide with no residue and no resistance pathway. Most failed fumigations are dose or sealing failures rather than resistance, and for export flour the binding constraint is residue limits, not pest mortality.

## The species that matter

| Insect | Preferred conditions | Damage |
|---|---|---|
| Sitophilus granarius (granary weevil) | 25–30 °C, 14%+ | Larvae hollow out kernels |
| Rhyzopertha dominica (lesser grain borer) | 28–34 °C, dry-tolerant | Severe, can infest at 11% moisture |
| Tribolium castaneum (red flour beetle) | 30–35 °C | Secondary feeder, flour and grain |
| Oryzaephilus surinamensis (saw-toothed grain beetle) | 30–33 °C | Secondary, attacks damaged grain |
| Sitotroga cerealella (Angoumois grain moth) | 25–30 °C | Larvae inside kernels |
| Acarus siro (mites) | 20–25 °C, 15%+ | Musty taint, not visible until heavy |

Two of these — lesser grain borer and Angoumois moth — develop **inside** the kernel, which means surface treatments do not reach them. Only fumigation or heat does.

## Physical control first

| Measure | Effect |
|---|---|
| Cool below 15 °C | Stops reproduction in most species |
| Cool below 10 °C | Kills many species over weeks |
| Dry below 12.5% | Limits development of most species |
| Hermetic sealing | Depletes oxygen; insects die |
| Turning | Mechanical damage and disturbance |

Cooling and drying are the foundation. A mill that cools properly fumigates once a year; one that does not fumigates four times and still has complaints.

## Monitoring

| Method | Detects | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Pitfall traps | Mobile adults in the grain mass | Weekly |
| Probe traps | Adults deeper in the bulk | Weekly |
| Sticky traps on structure | Moths, crawling insects | Weekly |
| Sieving samples | All stages, quantitative | Monthly |
| Temperature cables | Hot spots (indirect) | Continuous |
| X-ray or cracking | Internal stages | Quarterly audit |

**Threshold for action: two or more insects per trap per week, or any live insect in a flour mill intake area.**

Zero tolerance in a flour mill is not paranoia — one beetle in the mill becomes an infestation in the spouting within a month, and a customer finding an insect in a bag of flour is an unrecoverable event.

## Phosphine fumigation

Phosphine (hydrogen phosphide) from aluminium or magnesium phosphide tablets is the standard treatment worldwide.

### Protocol

| Step | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Sealing | Gas-tight; pressure test to 500 Pa, hold 5 minutes |
| Dose | 1.5–2 g phosphine per m³ for grain |
| Temperature | Above 15 °C; below this, unreliable |
| Duration | 7 days minimum, 10 days below 20 °C |
| Concentration | Above 200 ppm throughout |
| Monitoring | Daily reading at 3+ points |
| Ventilation | Until below 0.3 ppm before entry |

### Why fumigations fail

| Cause | Share of failures |
|---|---|
| Poor sealing | 45% |
| Insufficient dose | 20% |
| Too short | 15% |
| Temperature too low | 12% |
| Genuine resistance | 8% |

Resistance is real and spreading, but it is blamed far more often than it occurs. The first question after a failed fumigation should be how well the [silo](/knowledge/silo-design-and-aeration/) sealed, not which chemical to switch to.

### Resistance management

| Practice | Effect |
|---|---|
| Never under-dose | Sub-lethal doses select for resistance |
| Always complete the full duration | Same |
| Rotate with controlled atmosphere | No chemical selection pressure |
| Keep records per silo | Identifies problem bins |
| Heat treatment where feasible | 55 °C for 24 h kills all stages |

## Residue limits: the actual constraint

| Market | Phosphine limit in grain |
|---|---|
| EU | 0.01 mg/kg |
| USA | 0.1 mg/kg |
| Japan | 0.01 mg/kg |
| Codex | 0.1 mg/kg |

For export flour, the buyer will test. A fumigation that killed every insect but left 0.05 mg/kg fails an EU contract.

Ventilation after fumigation must be verified by measurement, not by elapsed time.

## Alternative treatments

| Method | Cost | Residue | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Controlled atmosphere (CO₂/N₂) | High capex | None | Requires sealed silos |
| Heat treatment | High energy | None | 55 °C for 24 h; needs equipment |
| Diatomaceous earth | Low | None (inert) | Slow acting; reduces flowability |
| Insect growth regulators | Moderate | Trace | Doesn't kill adults |
| Cold storage | Low running cost | None | Climate-dependent |

For organic or EU-bound flour, controlled atmosphere is becoming the default despite the capital cost.

## Mill hygiene

Storage pests become mill pests through the intake conveyor. Physical exclusion:

- Intake pit cleaned weekly, no grain residue
- Conveyor inspection hatches opened monthly
- Spouting joints sealed; flour accumulations vacuumed
- Pheromone traps throughout the mill, checked weekly
- Dead-leg spouting eliminated at design stage

The cheapest pest control in a flour mill is a design that has no dead ends where flour can accumulate.


## Reference points

The [FAO's manual on grain storage](https://www.fao.org/4/x5467e/x5467e00.htm) remains the most practical single document on small-to-medium storage, and its treatment of aeration and fumigation sealing is still accurate decades after publication.

Residue limits change, so check the current Codex or destination-market schedule rather than any figure quoted in an article — including this one.
