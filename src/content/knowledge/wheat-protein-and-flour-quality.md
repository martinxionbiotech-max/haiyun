## Quantity versus quality

| Dimension | Measured by | Controlled by |
|---|---|---|
| Protein content | Kjeldahl, NIR | Wheat selection and blending |
| Gluten strength | Farinograph stability, extensograph resistance | Wheat variety and growing conditions |
| Gluten extensibility | Extensograph extensibility | Wheat variety |
| Damaged starch | SDSS, iodine | Milling (roll pressure) |

A wheat can be 13% protein and still make poor bread if the gluten is weak and extensible. Conversely, a 11.5% wheat with strong, balanced gluten can make excellent bread.

Protein content is the number on the contract. Gluten quality is what the baker experiences.

## What milling can change

| Lever | Effect on protein |
|---|---|
| Stream selection (patent vs clear) | ±0.3–0.5 points |
| Extraction rate | Higher extraction = slightly lower protein |
| Roll pressure (damaged starch) | No effect on protein, large effect on behaviour |
| Blending at intake | Unlimited, within wheat available |

Stream selection works because protein is not distributed evenly. The aleurone layer and subaleurone cells are protein-rich, so streams that carry more of these run higher protein. Patent streams from the centre of the endosperm run lower.

In practice, most mills use blending rather than stream selection to hit a protein target, because stream selection sacrifices extraction.

## Blending arithmetic

Target: 12.0% protein from two wheats.

| Wheat | Protein | Available |
|---|---|---|
| A | 13.5% | 60% |
| B | 10.0% | 40% |

Blend protein = (0.6 × 13.5) + (0.4 × 10.0) = 8.1 + 4.0 = **12.1%**

To hit exactly 12.0%: solve 13.5x + 10.0(1−x) = 12.0 → x = 0.571. So 57.1% of wheat A.

Electronic proportioning scales do this continuously against a target, correcting for incoming variation. Manual blending cannot hold ±0.2 points; automatic can.

## Why ±0.2 points matters

A bakery running a fixed recipe on 12.0% protein flour will see:

| Delivered protein | Consequence |
|---|---|
| 12.0% | Reference |
| 11.7% | Weaker dough, lower volume, possible collapse |
| 12.3% | Tighter dough, reduced extensibility, shaping problems |
| Below 11.5% | Rejected by most industrial buyers |

Industrial bakeries run tight recipes. A half-point swing is a complaint; a full point is a rejected load.

## Damaged starch: the mill-controlled variable

Roll pressure damages starch granules, making them absorb water and feed yeast. Some damage is desirable; too much is not.

| Application | Target damaged starch |
|---|---|
| Bread | 6–9% |
| Noodle | 4–6% |
| Biscuit | 2–4% |
| Cake | 2–3% |

Damaged starch is controlled by reduction roll pressure and differential. Biscuit flour requires deliberately gentle reduction — which lowers output and requires wider roll gaps.

This is a genuine trade-off: you cannot produce both high-damage bread flour and low-damage biscuit flour from the same flow sheet without changing settings.

## Testing

| Test | Measures | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Kjeldahl | Protein content | Every wheat intake lot |
| NIR | Protein content | Hourly if available |
| Wet gluten | Quantity and approximate quality | Daily |
| Farinograph | Behaviour | On wheat change |
| Extensograph | Behaviour | On wheat change |
| SDSS / damaged starch | Starch damage | Monthly |

## The honest limitation

If your local wheat is 9.5% protein and your market wants bread flour, you must import high-protein wheat to blend. There is no milling solution.

This is a supply-chain decision that must be made before the plant is designed — because importing wheat changes your intake capacity, storage requirement, cleaning line and blending system.
