## Why this matters more than the machine

Two 1,000 mm roll mills from the same factory, same motor, same gap setting, will produce measurably different flour if one has fresh flutes and the other has 18 months of wear. The machine is a frame and a drive. The rolls do the work.

Yet fluting specification is usually left to the supplier, never revisited, and never measured.

## The four parameters

### 1. Flute pitch (flutes per inch)

Pitch sets the scale of the grinding action.

| Application | Flutes per inch | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1st break, hard wheat | 8–10 | Deep, aggressive, maximum scratch |
| 1st break, soft wheat | 10–12 | Shallower, less bran shattering |
| 2nd–3rd break | 10–14 | Progressive reduction |
| Coarse reduction | 14–18 | Shear-dominant |
| Fine reduction | 18–24 | Minimal scratch, high shear |
| Fine reduction, smooth | 0 (frosted) | Pure attrition |

The rule: **pitch increases as the stock gets finer.** Break rolls cut and scrape; reduction rolls shear and rub.

### 2. Flute profile

| Profile | Included angle | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Sharp-to-sharp | 90–100° | Breaks, maximum cutting |
| Sharp-to-dull | 90°/110° | Transition passages |
| Dull-to-dull | 110–120° | Reductions, more rubbing |
| Frosted / sandblasted | n/a | Fine reduction, no cutting edge |

Sharp-to-sharp with fresh flutes on first break produces the cleanest separation of bran from endosperm, but only if the gap is right. A sharp roll with too tight a gap shatters bran into the semolina and costs you ash.

### 3. Spiral

The helix angle, expressed as a percentage of roll circumference — typically 4–10%.

| Spiral | Effect |
|---|---|
| 0% (straight) | Uniform wear line, poor stock distribution |
| 4–6% | Standard, good compromise |
| 8–10% | Better wear distribution, slightly reduced shear |
| Opposed spirals (pair) | Pulls stock inward, better feed distribution |

Spiral matters for wear life more than for flour quality. A straight-fluted roll develops a wear groove that ruins the whole roll for one bad section.

### 4. Surface hardness

| Material | Hardness | Life | Cost index |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chilled cast iron | 450–520 HB | Baseline | 100 |
| Alloy chilled iron (Ni-Cr-Mo) | 500–560 HB | 1.3–1.6× | 130 |
| Centrifugally cast, composite | 550–600 HB shell | 1.8–2.2× | 175 |
| Tungsten carbide coated | 1,100+ HV | 3–5× | 300+ |

For a 200 t/d plant running hard wheat, the composite roll pays back in under three years on re-fluting cost and downtime alone. For a 20 t/d soft wheat mill, chilled iron is the rational choice.

## Specifying a roll set

A complete specification looks like this:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Roll length | 1,000 mm |
| Roll diameter | 250 mm |
| Flutes per inch | 10 |
| Profile | Sharp-to-sharp |
| Spiral | 6% |
| Material | Alloy chilled iron, 520 HB |
| Surface | Fluted, no frosting |
| Passage | 1st break |

Write it per passage. A flow sheet with 4B5M1S1T has 11 roll passages, each with a different ideal specification.

## Diagnosing wear

Rolls do not fail suddenly. They degrade in ways that are visible in the data long before they are visible to the eye.

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Extraction falls 1–2 points over months | Flutes worn, cutting action lost |
| Ash rises at constant extraction | Bran shattering from dull or over-tight rolls |
| Throughput falls at same gap | Flute depth worn, stock not being drawn in |
| Flour granulation coarsens | Reduction rolls need re-fluting or frosting |
| Uneven flour across the roll length | Spiral wear groove or roll out of parallel |
| Vibration and noise | Bearing or roll balance, not fluting |

### Measuring wear

| Method | Frequency | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Flute depth gauge | Quarterly | Direct wear measurement |
| Visual inspection with magnification | Monthly | Edge rounding, chipping |
| Extraction trend | Continuous | Aggregate performance |
| Granulation curve | Weekly | Whether the passage is doing its job |
| Power draw per passage | Continuous | Grinding resistance |

A flute depth loss of 0.15 mm on a 10-flute roll is roughly 20% of the working depth. That is the point at which re-fluting becomes economic.

## Re-fluting economics

| Option | Cost (1,000 mm roll) | Downtime | Performance restored |
|---|---|---|---|
| Re-flute existing | USD 350–700 | 3–7 days per set | 85–95% |
| Replace with new chilled iron | USD 3,500–6,000 | 1–3 days | 100% |
| Replace with composite | USD 6,000–11,000 | 1–3 days | 100%, longer life |

Re-fluting removes material from the roll diameter. A 250 mm roll can typically be re-fluted three to five times before the diameter is below the minimum for the bearing housing and gap adjustment range.

**Rule: budget re-fluting at 1.5–2.5% of plant capital per year.** A 60 t/d plant with USD 900,000 of equipment should carry USD 14,000–22,000 annually for roll maintenance. Mills that do not budget this end up running dull rolls for two extra years and losing 2–3 points of extraction the whole time — which costs far more than the re-fluting would have.

## Gap setting is not a substitute

A common error: as flutes wear, operators close the gap to restore throughput. This increases power draw, shatters bran, raises ash and accelerates wear further. The roll is not being restored, it is being abused.

The correct response to falling throughput is to measure flute depth. If the flutes are worn, re-flute. Closing the gap treats a symptom and creates three.

## Frosted rolls

Fine reduction passages (usually 8M onward) often use frosted rather than fluted rolls — a roughened surface produced by sandblasting or electrical discharge.

| Property | Effect |
|---|---|
| No cutting edge | Pure shear and attrition |
| Lower starch damage | Important for biscuit and cake flours |
| Lower power draw | 10–20% less than fluted at same gap |
| Shorter life | Surface roughness wears faster than flutes |

If your flour specification includes a starch damage limit — biscuit flour typically caps at 7–8% — frosted rolls are how you meet it.
