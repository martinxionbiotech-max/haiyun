Every milling project budget includes rolls. Almost none include the second set, and the second set arrives sooner than buyers expect.

This is the largest recurring capital item in a flour mill after wheat, and it is the one most often discovered as an emergency rather than planned as a cycle.

## How fast they actually wear

Chilled cast iron break rolls lose their cutting edge through a combination of abrasion and fatigue at the flute tip. The rate depends on three variables: wheat hardness, roll pressure setting, and how well the feed is distributed across the roll face.

| Duty | Operating hours to reflute or replace |
|---|---|
| Break rolls, hard wheat, 4B | 400–700 |
| Break rolls, soft wheat, 4B | 800–1,400 |
| Reduction rolls, smooth, hard wheat | 1,500–2,500 |
| Reduction rolls, smooth, soft wheat | 3,000–4,500 |

For a [60 t/d](/flour-milling-plant/40-60tpd-flour-plant/) mill running two shifts, 700 hours is about seven months. So the first set of break rolls needs attention inside the first year — and the quotation almost never says so.

## What wear actually costs, and why nobody sees it

Worn break flutes do not stop the mill. This is the whole problem.

A blunt roll still turns, still grinds, still produces flour. What it stops doing is scraping endosperm cleanly off the bran. The consequences accumulate in ways that look like other problems:

- Extraction falls one to three points
- Specific power rises, because the mill is grinding material it should have separated
- Break flour ash climbs, because more bran is being pulverised into the flour stream
- Sifter loading rises, because the stock is finer and flatter

A miller sees lower extraction and blames the wheat. A quality manager sees higher ash and blames the sifter. Nobody looks at the rolls, because the rolls are still turning.

The commercial scale: three points of extraction on a 60 t/d plant is about 1.8 tonnes of flour a day, roughly USD 700 a day at USD 400 a tonne. Against a refluting cost of a few thousand dollars, the payback on noticing is measured in a week.

## Measure the profile, not the diameter

The intuitive check — measure the roll diameter and compare with the nameplate — is nearly useless.

A roll can be at full diameter and completely blunt. What wears is the flute tip geometry: the sharp edge that does the cutting rounds over into a smooth land. Diameter tells you how much material remains for future reflutes; it tells you nothing about whether the roll still cuts.

Practical field checks, in order of reliability:

**The fingernail test.** Drag a fingernail across the flute at the discharge end. A sharp flute catches. A blunt one slides. Crude, and surprisingly decisive.

**The chalk test.** Chalk a section of the roll face, run the mill empty for a few revolutions, inspect. A sharp flute wipes the chalk cleanly; a worn one smears it.

**Extraction trend.** The most reliable of all, if you keep the record. Plot extraction against wheat source. When extraction drops while wheat is stable, look at the rolls.

What I would actually recommend: a **roll log** started at commissioning, with hours run per passage and the date of each inspection. It costs a page and it turns an emergency into a schedule.

## Reflute or replace

Refluting regrinds the flute profile into the existing chill, removing 2–4 mm of diameter per pass.

The arithmetic that matters is not the cost of one reflute but the total remaining life. A new roll with, say, 12 mm of usable chill depth supports two, possibly three reflutes before the diameter falls below what the mill stand can accommodate. So:

| Option | Cost | Remaining life |
|---|---|---|
| New roll | 100% | Full |
| First reflute | 25–35% | 60–70% |
| Second reflute | 25–35% | 30–40% |
| Third reflute | Usually not possible | — |

Two reflutes cost 50–70% of a new roll and deliver less than a new roll's life. That is why the honest general answer is: **reflute once, replace the second time.** Refluting is a way of bridging a cash-flow gap, not a maintenance strategy.

There is one exception. If the wear is uneven across the roll face — which happens when feed distribution is poor — refluting restores concentricity as well as profile, and that is worth doing even at a poor financial ratio.

## What to do before the first tonne

Ask for the roll specification in writing: chill depth, hardness, flute profile by passage, and the supplier's expected hours to reflute for your wheat.

A supplier who cannot answer that has not thought about your wheat. A supplier who answers "many years" is telling you they do not know.

Then put a line in the five-year plan for the second set of break rolls. It is coming, it is expensive, and the mills that budget for it treat it as maintenance. The mills that do not treat it as a crisis in the middle of a harvest.
