Every flour mill project has two halves. The first is a commercial negotiation about machines and money. The second is a construction project, and it is the second half that runs late.

The reason is structural rather than technical. A milling supplier sells equipment; a civil contractor builds a building; an electrical contractor brings power; a safety consultant writes the dust hazard analysis. Nobody in that chain owns the seams between them, and seams are where schedules die.

This handbook covers that second half — the safety obligations you inherit as an operator, the erection sequence, and the acceptance test that determines whether the plant you paid for is the plant you received.

## The interface problem

Here is the failure we see most often, and it has nothing to do with machinery quality.

The supplier quotes a storey-plant with a 27.5 m total height. The buyer's civil contractor builds to 26 m because a drawing revision was not passed on. The plant arrives, the top floor sifter cannot be installed, and everyone discovers that the contract never specified who checks drawing revisions against the as-built structure.

| Interface | Usually assumed by | Actually belongs to |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation loads and anchor bolt positions | Supplier | Supplier, but buyer must build to it |
| Floor flatness and level tolerance | Nobody | Buyer — and it matters more than most buyers believe |
| Power to the main panel | Buyer | Buyer, with supplier stating the kVA requirement |
| Compressed air capacity | Nobody | Buyer |
| Dust hazard analysis | Nobody | Buyer, as the operator |
| Lifting access for erection | Supplier | Buyer must provide the crane route |

Four of those six are "nobody" or misassigned on a typical contract. Each one becomes a change order.

## What safety obligations you inherit

An operator inherits obligations that the equipment supplier cannot discharge on their behalf, regardless of what the quotation implies.

The dust hazard analysis is the clearest example. It must cover the specific building, the specific equipment layout and the specific housekeeping regime — none of which the supplier controls. Insurers increasingly ask for it before binding cover, and it is the document that turns generic safety advice into a capital plan.

Electrical safety follows the same logic. Lockout-tagout procedures, restricted-space entry for silos and bins, and permit-to-work systems for hot work are operator systems. No supplier can install them.

A closing observation. Safety spending in a flour mill is judged by an event that mostly does not happen, which makes it permanently vulnerable to budget cuts. The mills that cut it are indistinguishable from the mills that did not — until one of them is not. That asymmetry is the whole argument, and it is worth remembering when a quotation line item looks easy to delete.
