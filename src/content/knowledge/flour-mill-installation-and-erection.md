Installation is the part of a milling project where the money is already committed and the control has already passed to other people.

By the time steel arrives, the buyer has signed, the supplier has been paid a deposit, and the schedule is in the hands of a civil contractor who has never built a mill. That is the structural weakness of every turnkey project, and no contract wording fully fixes it.

What you can do is understand the sequence, own the interfaces explicitly, and budget for the discoveries.

## The sequence, and why order matters

A storey-type plant erects **top-down** on the sifter floor and works downward. The reason is simple: the plansifter is the heaviest single item and it sits at the top. If you erect the lower floors first, you cannot get the sifter past them.

A floor-mounted plant — the [20–60 t/d](/flour-milling-plant/40-60tpd-flour-plant/) range with stand-alone sifters — erects **bottom-up** in flow order, because nothing is too large to move.

Getting this backwards is a common and expensive error. A contractor who erects a storey plant from the ground up will reach the fourth floor, discover the sifter cannot be lifted through the openings, and either cut the structure or hire a larger crane and remove a roof section.

| Phase | Duration (60 t/d storey plant) | Who owns it |
|---|---|---|
| Foundations and anchor bolts | 30–45 days | Buyer |
| Building shell and floors | 45–70 days, overlaps | Buyer |
| Steel erection, top-down | 25–35 days | Supplier, buyer provides crane |
| Spouting and ducting | 15–20 days | Supplier |
| Electrical and panel | 10–15 days | Buyer's contractor, supplier specifies |
| Pneumatic and compressed air | 5–10 days | Split — see below |
| Commissioning | 20–25 days | Supplier |

The overlaps are where the schedule is won or lost. Foundations, building and erection can run in parallel if the supplier issues anchor bolt drawings before the building design is frozen. They cannot if the buyer waits for a complete machine layout first.

## The four interfaces nobody owns

Every turnkey contract lists what the supplier delivers. Very few list what happens between the supplier's scope and the buyer's.

These four are the ones that appear as change orders:

**Compressed air.** The pneumatic conveying system, the filter receivers, the packer and the dampener all need air. The supplier specifies flow and pressure. Nobody specifies who buys the compressor, the dryer and the receiver tank — and a mill without a dryer will put water into its own air lines in a humid climate.

**Floor flatness.** Roller mills must sit level to within a tolerance that concrete contractors do not normally work to. A 3 mm fall across a mill floor is invisible to a builder and enough to make a roll gap drift.

**Crane access.** A storey plant needs a crane with reach over the building, not beside it. Site access roads, overhead power lines and neighbouring structures decide whether that is possible, and this is discovered at the worst moment.

**Power to the main panel.** The supplier states a kVA requirement and a cable size. The buyer runs the cable. The distance from the transformer to the panel is the buyer's information, and it changes the cable size, and nobody asks until the panel is on site.

Write all four into the contract with dates, not descriptions. "Buyer provides compressed air at 7 bar, 1.2 m³/min, at the plant room wall, by day 60" is enforceable. "Buyer provides compressed air" is not.

## The 8% that is always there

Ask any experienced project manager what contingency a milling installation needs and the answer converges on 5–8% of plant value. Not for machinery — for the things that appear once you are inside a real building.

The list is predictable in nature and unpredictable in detail:

- Steelwork that fouls a beam nobody measured
- A floor opening in the wrong place
- A drain where the panel needs to go
- A wall that is 200 mm out of plumb over four storeys
- A door too narrow for the purifier

None of these are supplier errors. They are the normal friction between a designed plant and an existing structure. Buyers who budget zero for them do not avoid the cost; they just discover it as a variation with a margin attached.

## What I would insist on

Three things, and they cost almost nothing.

**Anchor bolt drawings before the foundation design is frozen.** This single document prevents the most expensive class of installation error.

**A dated interface schedule signed by both parties.** Not a scope list — a schedule with days on it. Whoever misses a date owns the delay.

**A pre-erection site survey by the supplier's supervisor.** Two days of a supervisor's time, before the crane arrives. It finds the beam, the drain and the door.

The mills that go in smoothly are not the ones with better machinery. They are the ones where somebody walked the building with a tape measure before anyone poured concrete.
