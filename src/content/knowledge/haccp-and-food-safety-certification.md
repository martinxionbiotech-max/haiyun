HACCP is the part of a milling project that gets deferred until a customer asks for a certificate, at which point it becomes urgent, expensive and badly done.

The reason is that most guidance on HACCP is written for food manufacturing with many ingredients, many process steps and many genuine biological hazards. A flour mill is not that. It takes one agricultural raw material, applies physical processes, and produces a dry product with low water activity.

Which means the hazard analysis is genuinely simple — and most flour mill HACCP plans are far more complicated than the process they describe.

## The hazard profile is narrow

Wheat flour has almost no microbiological risk in the normal sense. Low moisture, low water activity, and a process that generates heat all work against pathogen proliferation. The hazards that matter are physical and chemical.

| Hazard class | Real examples in a flour mill |
|---|---|
| Physical | Metal from worn machinery, stone fragments, plastic from bags and sieves |
| Chemical | Mycotoxins, pesticide residues, cleaning chemical carryover, ink from packaging |
| Biological | Storage pests and their frass; Salmonella in exceptional moisture events |
| Allergen | Wheat itself, plus cross-contact in multigrain or shared lines |

That is the list. It is short, and the shortness is the point.

## The two real CCPs

**Metal detection on finished product.** This is the non-negotiable one, because the mill is full of wearing steel — roll chills, sifter frames, spouting, screen mesh, screws. A detector with a documented test protocol, a defined reject mechanism and a log that is actually completed is what a customer audit will check first.

The failure mode is not the absence of a detector. It is a detector that has never been challenged with test pieces at the specified interval, or a reject chute that puts rejected product back into the stream.

**[Mycotoxin](/knowledge/mycotoxin-control-in-flour-milling/) rejection at intake**, where the destination market sets limits. The control is the intake test and the rejection decision, not the cleaning process. If you have not defined a rejection threshold and given somebody the authority to reject a load, you do not have a control point — you have a hope.

Two more that sometimes qualify: magnets before the mill (if you treat them as a control rather than a precaution) and screen integrity checks on sifters, where a torn mesh releases product that should have been rejected.

## What is not a CCP

Almost everything else, and this is where plans go wrong.

Pest control, hygiene, training, maintenance, supplier approval, traceability, calibration, glass and brittle plastic registers — these are **prerequisite programmes**. They are mandatory, they are audited, and they keep the CCPs meaningful. They are not control points, because there is no critical limit you can monitor continuously and correct at the moment of failure.

A plan that lists pest control as CCP 3 tells an auditor that whoever wrote it did not understand the difference. It also guarantees that nobody monitors it properly, because a CCP log with twelve entries gets filled in at the end of the shift from memory.

## Where audits actually focus

A customer or certification audit follows the product, and the product spends most of its life in three places: the silo, the packing line, and the warehouse.

That is where the auditor will stand. Specifically:

- Silo condition, including the roof underside and any evidence of leakage
- Intake records and rejection records — do rejections exist, or has every load ever received been accepted?
- Packing area hygiene, bag storage, and whether the date coder can be bypassed
- Allergen changeover records, if you run more than one product
- Traceability: pick a bag, find the wheat. If that takes more than four hours, the system is theoretical

The mill floor itself gets a walk-through and a conversation about maintenance. The packing hall gets an hour.

## The honest summary

Build the prerequisite programmes first and properly: pest control with documented monitoring, a glass and brittle plastic register, calibration records, hygiene schedules, supplier approval for wheat and packaging. Get three months of records. Then write a short HACCP plan with two or three CCPs that people actually monitor.

The mills that fail audits are almost never the ones with weak hazard analysis. They are the ones with an elaborate plan and no records, or a [metal detector](/equipment/magnetic-separator/) nobody has challenged since installation.

One more thing, and it is the part that annoys quality managers: the cheapest route to passing an audit is not documentation, it is a clean packing hall and a filled-in metal detector log. Auditors are experienced people looking at physical evidence. Paperwork that describes a plant nobody maintains is transparently paperwork.
