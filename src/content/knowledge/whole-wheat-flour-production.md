True whole wheat is 100% of the cleaned kernel; commercial 'whole wheat' usually runs 85–95% because some bran is removed to keep the flour handleable and the shelf life survivable. The defining constraint is not extraction but rancidity — the germ's oils go off in three to six months at ambient temperature.

## What "whole wheat" actually means

Legally and technically: flour made from the entire cleaned kernel — endosperm, bran and germ in their original proportions.

In practice, commercial whole wheat runs 85–95% extraction. The missing 5–15% is the coarsest bran, removed because:

- It is difficult to sift and conveys poorly
- It makes flour feel gritty in some markets
- Removing it slightly extends shelf life

If your label claims 100% whole grain, you must retain everything. Verify your actual extraction before printing claims.

## The germ problem

The germ is 2–3% of the kernel and contains 10–12% oil, high in unsaturated fat and lipase enzymes.

Two mechanisms shorten shelf life:

1. **Lipase hydrolysis** — enzymes split triglycerides into free fatty acids, which taste soapy
2. **Lipid oxidation** — unsaturated fats react with oxygen, producing rancid flavours

Both accelerate with heat, moisture and oxygen exposure. Whole wheat flour at 25 °C in a paper bag is rancid in three months.

## Shelf-life strategies

| Strategy | Effect | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Stabilise germ by heat treatment | +3–6 months | Equipment + energy |
| Nitrogen flush packaging | +3–6 months | Gas + packaging |
| Refrigerated distribution | +3–6 months | Cold chain |
| Smaller pack sizes | Faster turnover | Higher packaging per kg |
| Short local supply chain | Days not months | Distribution discipline |
| Remove part of the germ | +2–4 months | Loses "whole grain" claim |

Most commercial whole wheat producers combine two: smaller packs and a local supply chain, with nitrogen flushing for retail.

## Stone versus roller for whole wheat

| Criterion | Stone | Roller |
|---|---|---|
| Bran flake size | Large | Small (unless specially set) |
| Gluten damage from bran | Less | More |
| Bread volume | Better | Lower |
| Flour temperature | Low | Higher |
| Output | Low | High |
| Consistency | Medium | High |
| Cost per tonne | Higher | Lower |

The bran flake size is the technical differentiator. Large flakes are visible in the crumb but do not sever the gluten network the way fine bran powder does. This is why stone-milled whole wheat bread often has noticeably better volume at the same flour specification.

## Process choices

**Option 1 — Straight stone grinding.** Whole kernel into stone, flour out. Simplest, produces the best-textured wholemeal, lowest throughput.

**Option 2 — Roller with all streams recombined.** [Roller mill](/equipment/fmfq-double-roller-mill/) separates streams then remixes them. High output, consistent, but bran is finely divided.

**Option 3 — Hybrid (SFM-1+2).** Roller splits and extracts core, stones grind, bran recombined at controlled particle size. Balances texture and throughput.

**Option 4 — Reconstituted.** Mill white flour, then add back bran and germ in measured proportions. Technically legal in many jurisdictions if labelled correctly, and clearly inferior in baking behaviour. Avoid claiming "stone ground" if you do this.

## Handling and storage

- Store below 20 °C where possible
- Keep sealed — whole wheat absorbs moisture and odours faster than white flour
- Rotate stock strictly: first in, first out, with date coding
- Clean silos and bins between lots — residual germ oil oxidises and taints new stock
