Ten stages in a fixed order, and the order is physical rather than customary: coarse before fine, dry before wet, size before density, magnets last. Reorder any two and you will blind a screen or send stones into a roll. The sequence is not a convention you can improve on with a clever layout.

This article describes **the sequence** — the ten stages in order, and why reordering them breaks separation. It does not size anything. For machine selection, capacity rules, and the wet-versus-dry capital decision, see [wheat cleaning system design](/knowledge/wheat-cleaning-system-design/).

## The ten stages

| # | Stage | Machine | Removes | Skip it and… |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Intake & weighing | Weighbridge, scale | Nothing — measures | You cannot control wheat cost |
| 2 | Coarse pre-cleaning | Drum sieve | Straw, rope, cob, plastic | Ropes wrap shafts; screens blind |
| 3 | Size grading | Vibrating screen | Large and small impurities | Sand enters the destoner and reduces its efficiency |
| 4 | Density separation | Gravity destoner | Stones, glass, mud balls | Stones reach the rolls and the flour |
| 5 | Surface scouring | Horizontal scourer | Dirt, beard, smut, bacteria | Ash rises 0.03–0.06 points |
| 6 | Length grading (optional) | Indent cylinder | Broken, shrivelled, diseased kernels | Ash rises at the top end |
| 7 | Wet cleaning (optional) | Washer-destoner-dryer | Crease dirt, residue, stones | Residue limits may not be met |
| 8 | Dampening | Automatic dampener | Nothing — adds water | Bran shatters, extraction falls |
| 9 | Magnetic separation | Plate / drum magnets | Ferrous metal | Roll damage and recall risk |
| 10 | Aspiration | Aspirator | Final chaff and beard | Contaminants enter the mill |

## Why the order is fixed

Four physical rules govern the sequence:

**Coarse before fine.** Large material must leave before any fine screen, otherwise it bridges and blinds.

**Dry before wet.** Wet cleaning makes wheat sticky. Running a wet stream through a dry screen destroys separation.

**Size before density.** A destoner works on material of similar size. Un-graded wheat with large stones and fine sand cannot be stratified properly.

**Magnets last.** You want to catch wear particles generated by the machines upstream, not just the ones that arrived in the truck.

## Cleaning versus pre-cleaning capacity

Every machine datasheet shows two numbers. On a TQLZ100×150 they are 50 t/h and 12 t/h.

The first is how fast you can scalp coarse material. The second is how fast you can actually clean. The ratio is roughly 4:1 because real cleaning requires far longer residence time on the deck.

**Size on the cleaning figure.** This is the most common specification error in the industry.

## Where the flow differs by capacity

| Capacity | Scourer passes | Length grader | Wet cleaning | Magnets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 t/d | 1 | Optional | Rare | 3 |
| 30 t/d | 1–2 | Optional | Optional | 3–4 |
| 60 t/d | 2 | Common | Common | 4–5 |
| 100 t/d | 2 + brush | Standard | Standard | 5–6 |
| 300 t/d+ | 2 + brush | Standard | Standard | 6–7 |

Below 30 t/d most plants omit wet cleaning and length grading. Above 60 t/d both become standard because the quality delta is worth more than the capital.


## Further reading on the sequence

The [UK Flour Millers](https://www.ukflourmillers.org/) technical pages cover the same cleaning sequence from the trade body's perspective, and are a useful cross-check that the order described here is standard practice rather than one supplier's habit.

The one place suppliers genuinely differ is how many scourer passes they specify. Two is the norm above 60 t/d; one is common below 30 t/d and is usually enough for domestic commodity markets.
