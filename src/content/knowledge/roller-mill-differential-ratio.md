Break rolls run at 2.5:1 to open the kernel and scrape endosperm off bran; reduction rolls step from 2.0:1 down to 1.25:1 so they reduce by attrition rather than cutting. Set one position wrong and you generate heat, fine bran and ash with no obvious mechanical symptom — the machine sounds perfectly healthy while it damages your product.

## What the differential does

Two rolls turn at different speeds. The slow roll grips the material; the fast roll does work on it.

The ratio determines **how** the work happens:

| Differential | Action | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| 2.5 : 1 | Shear — the kernel is held and torn open | Break passages 1B–4B |
| 2.0 : 1 | Shearing with more crushing | Scratch, early reduction |
| 1.5 : 1 | Crushing and rubbing | Mid reduction |
| 1.25 : 1 | Gentle attrition | Late reduction |

## Why breaks need high shear

A wheat kernel is held together by bran layers wrapped around the endosperm. To get endosperm out you must open the kernel, not crush it.

High differential with fluted rolls does exactly this: the flutes on the slow roll hold the kernel while the fast roll's flutes shear across it. The kernel splits along its natural seam and endosperm comes away in chunks.

Low differential would crush the kernel, breaking bran into powder that contaminates the endosperm. That is the failure mode of an incorrectly set break passage.

## Why reduction needs low differential

Reduction takes clean endosperm and makes it finer. There is no bran to avoid damaging — the goal is a controlled particle size distribution with minimal heat.

High differential would cut particles, concentrating energy in a small volume and generating heat. Heat degrades gluten and volatilises aromatics. It also generates additional fine bran from whatever contamination remains.

Low differential crushes gently across a larger contact area. Slower, cooler, cleaner.

## What wrong ratios look like

You will not see a mechanical symptom. You will see:

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Ash 0.05–0.10 above specification | Reduction roll differential too high |
| Flour temperature 8–15 °C above normal | Any roll too tight or differential too high |
| Visible bran specks in patent flour | Break differential too low — kernel crushed, not split |
| Rapid roll wear | Excessive pressure compensating for wrong differential |
| Extraction 2–4 points below design | Dull flutes on a break passage |

None of these triggers an alarm. All of them are diagnosed from ash data and temperature logs.

## Roll condition: the silent failure

Flutes wear. A worn flute becomes rounded and stops cutting — it **polishes** the bran instead, pressing it against the endosperm.

The mill keeps running. Power consumption may even fall slightly, because a polished roll does less work. Ash rises slowly, and by the time anyone notices, extraction has dropped 2–3 points.

| Operating hours | Flute condition | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1,000 | Sharp | — |
| 1,000–1,800 | Slightly rounded | Plan re-fluting |
| 1,800–2,500 | Rounded, polishing | Re-flute |
| > 2,500 | Smearing | Re-flute immediately |

Re-[fluting](/knowledge/roll-fluting-and-surface-technology/) costs a fraction of the losses. Schedule it by hours, not by symptom.

## Checking alignment

Roll parallelism matters as much as ratio. Check every 500 hours:

1. Insert a feeler gauge at both ends of the roll gap
2. Difference must be under 0.05 mm
3. A 0.05 mm taper produces a visible ash gradient across the flour stream — one side of the sifter runs higher ash than the other

Alignment drifts after roll changes and after bearing wear. Check after every roll change without exception.

## The four parameters that must be set together

| Parameter | Break | Reduction |
|---|---|---|
| Differential | 2.5:1 | 1.25–2.0:1 |
| Roll surface | Fluted (sharp) | Smooth (or fine fluted for scratch) |
| Roll gap | 0.4–0.8 mm progressive | 0.05–0.3 mm progressive |
| Pressure | Moderate | Light |

Change one and the others must be re-evaluated. This is why roll setting is a skilled job and why mills with high staff turnover produce inconsistent flour.
