Freshly milled flour bakes badly — tight, bucky dough that no formula change will fix — because it needs two to six weeks of oxidation first. Maturation changes gluten; bleaching only changes colour. Confusing the two produces flour that looks right on the shelf and performs wrong in the bakery.

## The freshness paradox

A mill that ships flour the day it is milled will receive complaints. The flour is analytically correct — right protein, right ash, right moisture — and it bakes badly.

The cause is oxidation chemistry.

### What happens in fresh flour

Freshly milled flour contains:

| Compound | Effect on gluten |
|---|---|
| Glutathione | Breaks disulfide bonds, weakens |
| Free cysteine | Blocks bond formation |
| Sulfhydryl groups on glutenin | Prevent cross-linking |
| Lipoxygenase activity | Variable, mostly negative |

The result: dough that is tight, bucky, lacks extensibility, and produces bread with poor volume and a dense crumb.

Over 2–6 weeks of storage in contact with air, these reducing compounds oxidise naturally. Sulfhydryl groups form disulfide bonds between glutenin chains, and the gluten network strengthens and becomes extensible.

The flour has not changed in composition. It has changed in oxidation state.

## Natural maturation

| Condition | Time to mature |
|---|---|
| 20–25 °C, aerated, in bags | 3–4 weeks |
| 15–20 °C, aerated | 4–6 weeks |
| Below 15 °C | 8+ weeks, slow |
| Sealed, no air | Very slow, months |
| Bulk silo with aeration | 2–3 weeks |

### Practical requirements

- **Storage capacity**: 2–4 weeks of production in bags or bulk
- **Aeration**: air contact is essential; sealed silos retard maturation
- **Temperature**: 18–25 °C is optimal; cold storage slows it
- **Rotation**: FIFO, with date marking

For a [60 t/d](/flour-milling-plant/40-60tpd-flour-plant/) mill, that means 840–1,680 t of finished flour storage. This is a real capital item that is frequently omitted from project budgets, and its absence is why new mills cannot sell their first month of production.

## Accelerated maturation

Where storage time is not available, chemical maturing agents oxidise the flour directly.

| Agent | Function | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Ascorbic acid (E300) | Oxidises sulfhydryls via dehydroascorbic acid | Permitted almost everywhere |
| Azodicarbonamide (ADA) | Fast oxidiser, strong dough strengthening | Banned in EU; permitted in US, limited elsewhere |
| Potassium bromate | Strong oxidiser | Banned in EU, Canada, China, many others |
| Calcium peroxide | Slow oxidiser, also aids drying | Permitted in some markets |
| L-cysteine | Reducing agent — the opposite effect | Used to relax dough |

### Ascorbic acid

The default choice for export markets because it is accepted nearly everywhere and carries no labelling burden beyond "flour treatment agent".

| Dose | Effect |
|---|---|
| 20–40 ppm | Mild strengthening, improved volume |
| 40–80 ppm | Noticeable dough strengthening |
| 80–150 ppm | Strong, for weak wheat |
| Above 150 ppm | Diminishing returns, possible over-oxidation |

Ascorbic acid requires oxygen and the enzyme ascorbate oxidase, both present in flour, so its action is gradual and self-limiting. That is why it is difficult to overdose and why it is the preferred agent.

### The regulatory map

| Market | Bleaching | Maturing |
|---|---|---|
| EU | Prohibited | Ascorbic acid only |
| UK | Prohibited | Ascorbic acid |
| USA | Benzoyl peroxide, chlorine, chlorine dioxide | ADA, ascorbic acid, bromate (state-dependent) |
| China | Permitted (specified list) | Ascorbic acid, ADA restricted |
| Middle East | Varies by country | Generally ascorbic acid |
| Japan | Restricted | Ascorbic acid |

For export flour, the destination market's rules govern. A mill supplying five countries needs five specifications, and getting this wrong is a rejected shipment, not a quality complaint.

## Bleaching

Bleaching targets pigments, not protein.

| Agent | Mechanism | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Benzoyl peroxide | Oxidises xanthophyll | White, neutral |
| Chlorine gas | Oxidises pigments + modifies starch | Very white, cake flour specific |
| Chlorine dioxide | Oxidises pigments | White |
| Azodicarbonamide | Incidental bleaching | White + strengthening |
| Natural ageing | Slow oxidation | Gradual whitening |

### Chlorine and cake flour

Chlorine treatment does something bleaching alone cannot: it modifies starch granule behaviour, lowering pH to 4.6–5.2 and changing the starch's gelatinisation and water absorption. This is why cake flour is chlorinated — the effect on crumb structure is not achievable any other way.

Chlorinated cake flour is standard in the US. It is prohibited in the EU, where cake flour is made from low-protein soft wheat without chlorination.

## Enrichment

Separate from both bleaching and maturation: restoring nutrients lost in milling.

| Nutrient | Typical addition (per kg flour) |
|---|---|
| Thiamin (B1) | 4.4–6.4 mg |
| Riboflavin (B2) | 2.6–4.0 mg |
| Niacin | 35–53 mg |
| Iron | 28–44 mg |
| Folic acid | 1.4–2.6 mg |
| Calcium | 1,100–1,400 mg (where mandated) |

Mandatory in the US, Canada, UK (folic acid), and around 80 other countries. Not mandatory in most of continental Europe.

| Requirement | Practical implication |
|---|---|
| Feeder accuracy ±2% | Volumetric feeders are insufficient; use gravimetric |
| Uniformity testing | Assay per batch, not per shift |
| Record keeping | Regulatory audit trail |
| Label declaration | "Enriched" or "Fortified" claims |

Enrichment equipment is a USD 8,000–30,000 addition and adds a regulatory compliance obligation that many mills are not staffed for.

## Heat treatment

A physical treatment with specific uses:

| Purpose | Temperature/time | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Microbial reduction | 70–90 °C, seconds | Reduces total plate count 2–4 logs |
| Enzyme inactivation | 80–100 °C | Stops lipase, extends shelf life |
| Gluten modification | 80–120 °C | Partially denatures, changes functionality |

Heat-treated flour is used in products where raw flour is a microbiological risk — ready-to-eat dough, some confectionery, certain baby foods. It is not a general-purpose treatment.

## The treatment decision tree

| Requirement | Solution |
|---|---|
| General bakery flour, market allows time | 3–4 weeks natural maturation |
| General bakery flour, fast turnaround | 30–60 ppm ascorbic acid |
| Export to EU | Ascorbic acid only, no bleaching |
| White noodle flour | Natural ageing or low-dose ascorbic acid; avoid bleaching |
| Cake flour, US market | Chlorinate |
| Cake flour, EU market | Soft wheat, no chlorination, heat treatment if microbial spec |
| Bread flour, weak wheat | 60–100 ppm ascorbic acid |
| Enriched flour, US/UK | Vitamin premix + gravimetric feeder |

## What this means for a mill project

Two items frequently missing from a new mill's budget:

1. **Finished flour storage** sized for 3–4 weeks of production, aerated and temperature-controlled
2. **A treatment system** — gravimetric feeder, premix handling, and the quality lab to verify it

A mill without the first cannot sell its first month of output at full value. A mill without the second cannot meet the specification of any customer who needs accelerated maturation, enrichment or bleaching.
