A mill is not a project. It is a factory that has to run for twenty years with people who were not in the room when it was designed.

That distinction is where most purchasing decisions go wrong. Buyers spend months on capacity, flow sheet and price, then sign a contract that says nothing about who stocks the sifter hanger bushes — and discover in year two that the answer is "you do, and they ship in eleven weeks."

This handbook covers the part after commissioning: what wears out, who watches it, what you keep on the shelf, and which automation is worth the money. It is deliberately opinionated. Where the industry has a comfortable answer that does not survive contact with a real mill, we say so.

## The three numbers that decide whether a mill runs

Forget OEE for a moment. Three simpler numbers tell you whether a plant will be profitable, and all three are visible within a month of startup.

| Number | Healthy | What it means when it is wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Hours between unplanned stops | 60+ | Below 40 you have a maintenance problem, not a production problem |
| Spare part lead time, worst item | < 2 weeks | Above 6 weeks, one bearing failure costs you a month |
| People who can set a roll gap | ≥ 2 | One person means your mill stops when they take leave |

Most buyers never ask any of these before signing. Every buyer asks about price per tonne.

## What actually breaks

The failure distribution in a wheat flour mill is remarkably consistent, and it is not where buyers expect.

Roller mill bearings and roll wear account for the largest share of unplanned downtime in every mill we have seen. Sifter hanger rods and screen frames come second. Pneumatic system valves and filter bags third. Electrical faults are rare but expensive when they happen, because diagnosis takes longer than repair.

The machinery that buyers worry about most — the [plansifter](/equipment/double-bin-plansifter/), the purifier, the roller mill chassis — is usually the machinery that gives least trouble. It is the consumables and the accessories that stop the plant.

## Reading this handbook

Four articles, in the order a new mill owner needs them:

- **[Preventive maintenance](/knowledge/flour-mill-preventive-maintenance-schedule/)** — the schedule, and the failure mode behind every interval in it.
- **Spare parts** — what to stock, what to order on demand, and the items that are never worth holding.
- **Staffing and training** — headcount by capacity, and the training that separates a mill running at design from one running at 70%.
- **Automation** — what to automate, what to leave alone, and why the labour-saving case is usually the weakest one.

One warning before you read any of them. Operational discipline is cheap and it is also the first thing cut when a mill is under financial pressure. A plant that stops greasing on schedule to save a fitter's hour will spend forty times that hour on a seized bearing. That is not a theory; it is the single most common cause of the emergencies we are called about.
