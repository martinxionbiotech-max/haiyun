Every mill owner knows wheat is 70–80% of the cost. Very few know what position they are holding in it.

That is the central fact of [wheat procurement](/knowledge/wheat-classes-and-specifications/): **by default, a flour mill is long wheat.** You buy the raw material, hold it in a silo, and sell the finished product later. If the wheat price falls between buying and selling, you lose — even if the mill ran perfectly and the flour was excellent.

The question is never whether to take price risk. It is whether you took it deliberately.

## Basis and flat, which are not the same thing

This distinction is the single most useful concept in grain procurement, and it is routinely collapsed into "the wheat price."

**Flat price** is the absolute number per tonne. It moves with global supply, currency, and speculative flow. A small mill cannot forecast it and should not try.

**Basis** is the difference between your local delivered price and a reference futures price. It reflects local logistics, local supply, quality spread and transport. Basis is what a mill can actually understand, because it is about the region it buys in.

| | Flat price | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| What moves it | World supply, currency, funds | Local crop, transport, quality |
| Can a mill forecast it | No | Yes, with local knowledge |
| Where a mill has edge | None | Considerable |
| Where small mills lose money | Almost always here | — |

A mill that "hedges" by watching the futures price and buying when it looks low is trying to trade flat price against professionals who do it full time. A mill that manages basis — buying when the local spread is wide, contracting transport in advance, knowing which elevator is short — is competing on information it actually has.

The practical implication: **manage basis, pass flat price through.** Any flour contract that does not let you pass flat price movement through is a contract in which you are the speculator.

## The storage-and-carry trap

The intuitive strategy is buy at harvest, when wheat is cheap, and mill it all year. It is also where a great deal of money disappears.

Harvest purchase only pays if the seasonal price rise exceeds the full cost of carrying. That cost has four parts and mills routinely count only the first:

| Cost component | Typical order of magnitude |
|---|---|
| Interest on the wheat value | 8–15% a year |
| Storage and handling | 2–4% a year |
| Shrinkage, moisture loss, pests | 0.5–2% a year |
| Quality deterioration | Unpriced, and real |

Add those up and the true carry is often 12–20% annualised. If the local seasonal spread is 8%, buying at harvest and holding is a losing trade that looks like a clever purchase.

This is not an argument against storage. It is an argument for sizing storage to the operation rather than to the harvest. A mill that stores three months of wheat is buying optionality — the sizing logic is set out in [silo design and aeration](/knowledge/silo-design-and-aeration/). A mill that stores eleven months is running a warehouse business with a milling plant attached, and usually without the skills for either.

## The clauses that shift risk to you

Wheat purchase contracts and flour sales contracts both contain terms that quietly allocate price risk. In our experience the flour side is where mills get hurt, because the wheat side is negotiated by someone who thinks about wheat and the flour side by someone who thinks about sales.

Watch for these:

**Fixed flour price for a fixed period with no adjustment.** A twelve-month fixed price on flour, against wheat bought monthly, is an unhedged short position in wheat of twelve months' duration. If wheat rises 20%, the mill absorbs it.

**Wheat quality clauses with rejection rights but no price adjustment.** The buyer can reject on protein or moisture, but the price does not move if the wheat is better than specified. All the upside goes one way.

**Delivery windows with penalty clauses.** A mill that commits to delivering flour in a specific week and takes wheat on a call-off basis carries the entire logistics risk between the two.

**"Market price at time of delivery" with no reference market named.** Unenforceable in practice, because there is no agreed number to point at.

**Force majeure drafted by the buyer's lawyer.** Read it. In a tight market, a supplier whose force majeure clause covers "any change in market conditions" has an option, not an obligation.

The remedy is unglamorous and it works: name the reference market, state the adjustment formula, cap the exposure, and put a duration on it. A flour price that adjusts monthly against a named wheat index is still a commercial negotiation, but at least both parties know what they are trading.

## What a small mill should actually do

Three things, in order of value per hour spent.

**Match the duration of your wheat position to your flour commitments.** The cost arithmetic is in [flour mill operating cost](/knowledge/flour-mill-operating-cost-breakdown/). If your flour contracts are monthly, do not hold nine months of wheat. If a customer wants an annual fixed price, buy the wheat forward for that volume before you sign — or price in a margin large enough to survive a bad year.

**Track basis weekly, and record it.** A notebook with the local delivered price and the reference futures price, every week, builds the only dataset that gives a small mill an information advantage. Nobody sells this data for your region.

**Do not hedge what you cannot measure.** Futures hedging requires margin, mark-to-market accounting and a discipline that most family-owned mills do not have. A mill that hedges badly is worse off than one that does not hedge and knows its exposure.

## The honest summary

The mills that survive price volatility are not the ones with the cleverest procurement strategy. They are the ones that know, at any moment, how many tonnes of wheat they own and at what price they have committed to sell the flour made from it.

That number — the open position — is the whole discipline. Everything else is commentary.

A mill owner who cannot state it in one sentence is not managing price risk. They are exposed to it, and the exposure was chosen by default rather than by decision.
