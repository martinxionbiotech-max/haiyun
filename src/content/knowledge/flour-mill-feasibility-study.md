Most feasibility studies are wrong in the same three places: they use national demand figures instead of the radius you can actually deliver to, they assume full capacity in year one, and they treat the equipment quotation as the project cost. Fix those three and the study becomes a decision tool rather than a funding document.

## Section 1 — Demand analysis (the one that matters)

Not national flour consumption. Your **distribution radius**.

| Question | Why |
|---|---|
| How many bakeries within 150 km? | Direct buyers |
| What do they pay now, and to whom? | Price benchmark |
| What is their current supply reliability? | Your entry point |
| How many households buy flour retail? | Retail channel |
| What are the import barriers and duties? | Competitive floor |
| Who are the incumbents and what is their spare capacity? | Real competition |

The last question kills more projects than any other. If three mills within your radius are running at 60% capacity, they will cut price rather than lose share.

## Section 2 — Wheat supply

| Question | Why |
|---|---|
| Where will wheat come from, season by season? | Continuity |
| What is the price history over 5 years? | Volatility risk |
| What is the quality (protein, moisture, impurity)? | Extraction rate |
| Can you contract forward? | Cost certainty |
| What storage do you need? | 30–45 days minimum |
| Is import required, and at what duty? | Cost floor |

A mill that sources wheat opportunistically will have a 6-point extraction swing between loads. That is a 6% revenue swing on 80% of your cost base.

## Section 3 — Technical design

Must include:

- Flow sheet with passage count
- Cleaning line with machine list
- Sifter area calculation
- Power budget
- Building dimensions
- Storage capacities
- Utility requirements (water, air, effluent)

If a feasibility study says "a [60 t/d](/flour-milling-plant/40-60tpd-flour-plant/) flour mill" without a flow sheet, it has not been designed — only specified.

## Section 4 and 5 — Capital and operating cost

Capital must include the second 55%: building, electrical, installation, working capital.

Operating must be built bottom-up from local prices, not from a template.

## Section 6 — Financial model

Build three scenarios:

| Scenario | Utilisation year 1 | Wheat price | Flour price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base | 65% | Current | Current |
| Downside | 45% | +10% | Current |
| Upside | 85% | Current | +3% |

If the downside scenario cannot service debt, the project is fragile regardless of the base case.

Include:

- Monthly cash flow for 24 months (construction + ramp-up)
- Working capital cycle
- Debt service coverage ratio
- Break-even utilisation
- Sensitivity table on wheat and flour price

## Section 7 — Risk register

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Wheat price spike | Forward contracts, multi-origin blending |
| Demand shortfall | Phased capacity, contract milling |
| Power interruption | Generator, UPS on controls |
| Key staff departure | Cross-training, documented procedures |
| Equipment failure | Spares inventory, service agreement |
| Regulatory change | Early permitting, compliance design |

## The five assumptions that sink studies

**1. 100% utilisation in year one.** Reality is 50–70%.

**2. National demand figures.** Your market is a radius, not a country.

**3. Wheat at today's price forever.** Model +20% at minimum.

**4. Equipment cost as project cost.** It is 63–66% of the project, not the project.

**5. Ignoring incumbent spare capacity.** The cheapest competitor sets your price.

## A simple sanity test

Before commissioning a study, answer these three questions:

1. Who are the five bakeries that will buy from me next month, by name?
2. Where does my wheat come from in month 7, after the harvest is sold?
3. What happens to my numbers if flour price falls 8%?

If you cannot answer all three, the study will produce a document rather than a decision.
